# 05 – Brakes (band, mechanical disc, hydraulic disc, sizing, bedding)

## Heavy driver notes (250 lb driver, ~450 lb all-up, 30 mph)
- Kinetic energy at 30 mph scales with mass: your kart stores ~45–50% more energy than the 300 lb kart the brake kit was designed around. Every stop puts that into the rotor and pads.
- **Band brakes are not acceptable** on this vehicle. A mechanical cable disc is the bare minimum for flat yard use; a **hydraulic disc on the live axle is the recommended baseline**, and front brakes or a second rear caliper are worth adding if you ride hills.
- Use the largest rotor that clears: 7.1" or 8" rather than 6". Rotor radius is free brake torque.
- Rear-only braking on a kart with ~60% rear weight bias is limited by rear tire grip to about 0.4–0.5 g on dirt. If you want to stop a 450 lb kart from 30 mph in under ~60 ft on grass, you need front brakes.
- Pedal: a 250 lb driver can push hard, but a long travel/high effort pedal invites a panic stomp that locks the rear and spins the kart. Size the master cylinder so a firm 60–90 lb push gives full braking.

## Brake types
| Type | How it works | Typical cost | Suitability for a 450 lb kart |
|---|---|---|---|
| Band brake | Steel band with lining squeezed around a 4" drum on the axle by a cable/rod | $20–35 | **Inadequate.** Fades hot, self-energizes inconsistently, cable stretch, drum ~4" dia = little torque. Forum consensus: fine for kids, not for adults. |
| Mechanical (cable) disc | Cable-pulled caliper (single moving pad, fixed pad) on a 6" rotor, 1" bore hub | $45–90 | Minimum. Clamp force limited by cable (~200–400 lb est.). Works for flat yard riding; marginal on hills. Upgrade to 7–8" rotor and a heavy-duty cable if you use it. |
| Hydraulic disc, single rear | Master cylinder + caliper on live-axle rotor | $100–350 | **Recommended baseline.** 3–5x the clamp force of a cable caliper, consistent pedal. |
| Hydraulic, dual rear (two calipers or two rotors) | Two calipers on one rotor, or rotor at each axle end | +$80–200 | Spreads heat; does not increase max decel (still limited by rear tire grip). Useful on long descents. |
| Hydraulic, front + rear | Rotors on both front hubs plus rear | +$150–400 | Best stopping; needs front hubs with rotor mounts and flexible lines through the steering travel. Worth it for hill riding at your weight. |

## Rotors, hubs and mounting
- Common sizes: **6"** (yard-kart kits, 1" bore), **7.1"** (MCP/racing, 4-bolt on 3" circle), **8"** (Yerf-Dog/Manco style, and generic 8" with 1" bore), 10" exists for buggies.
- Thickness: yard-kart rotors ~3/16" (.1875"); racing .125"–.25". The caliper must match the rotor thickness (Wilwood and MCP sell calipers per thickness; cheap mechanical calipers accept ~.18–.25").
- **Axle-mount rotor** (rotor has a 1" or 1-1/4" bore with key and set screws): cheapest; the rotor is the hub. Fine for 6" mechanical kits. Hard to replace without pulling the axle.
- **Hub-mount rotor** (separate keyed hub with a 3- or 4-bolt flange, rotor bolts on): preferred. Rotor changes are a 10-minute job and you can step up to a 7.1"/8" rotor on the same hub. For a 1-1/4" axle this is the normal style.
- Put the rotor **inboard of a bearing hanger**, not cantilevered past the last bearing, and within ~6" of the sprocket so the axle isn't twisted by brake torque at one end and drive torque at the other.
- Caliper bracket: 1/4" plate, welded to the frame rail (not to a bearing hanger alone). The bracket sees the full brake torque: at 225 ft-lb axle torque and a 3" lever to the mount, that is ~900 lb on the bracket bolts – use two 3/8" Grade 8 bolts minimum.
- Use a floating or slotted bracket so the caliper centers on the rotor; a rigid single-piston caliper that isn't centered drags one pad.

## Pedal ratio and line pressure basics
- Pedal ratio = distance from pedal pivot to foot pad ÷ distance from pivot to master cylinder pushrod. Kart pedals run **3:1 to 5:1**; 4:1 is a good target. Wilwood's kart master has an adjustable ratio built in.
- Line pressure (psi) = (pedal force x pedal ratio) ÷ master cylinder piston area.
  - 1/2" bore master: area 0.196 in². 60 lb foot x 4:1 = 240 lb → **~1,220 psi**.
  - 5/8" bore: area 0.307 in². Same input → ~780 psi.
  - 3/4" bore: area 0.442 in². Same input → ~540 psi (too low for one small caliper; OK for 3–4 calipers).
- Caliper clamp force = line pressure x total piston area on one side (x2 for opposed pistons). A Wilwood kart caliper with two 1.00" pistons (0.785 in² each) at 1,220 psi gives ~960 lb per pad.
- Smaller master bore = more pressure, more pedal travel. One rear caliper → 1/2"–5/8" master. Front + rear (3 calipers) → 5/8"–3/4" master, or two masters with a balance bar.
- Pedal travel check: total caliper piston displacement (area x ~0.040" pad travel per piston) should be less than ~40% of master cylinder stroke x area, or the pedal goes to the floor.

## Rough sizing calculation – 450 lb at 30 mph (all estimates)
1. Energy: v = 30 mph = 44 ft/s; m = 450/32.2 = 14.0 slug. KE = 0.5 x 14.0 x 44² ≈ **13,500 ft-lb** per stop. Dumped into a 6" steel rotor (~1.5 lb) that is a ~30 °F temperature rise per hard stop – single stops are fine, repeated downhill drags are what overheat small rotors.
2. Target deceleration: 0.6 g on pavement (19.3 ft/s²), realistically 0.4 g on grass/dirt (12.9 ft/s²).
   - Stopping distance = v²/(2a): 1,936/38.6 ≈ **50 ft at 0.6 g**, ≈ **75 ft at 0.4 g** (plus ~20 ft reaction at 30 mph).
3. Braking force at the tires = 0.6 x 450 = **270 lb**. With ~60% rear weight (270 lb on the rear tires) and dirt friction ~0.5, the rear can only supply ~135 lb → **rear-only ≈ 0.3 g on dirt, ~0.45 g on pavement**. That is the argument for front brakes.
4. Axle brake torque needed for 270 lb on a 20" tire (10" radius): 270 x 10 = 2,700 in-lb = **225 ft-lb**.
5. Clamp force needed: T = 2 x μ x F x r_eff, with pad μ ≈ 0.4 and r_eff ≈ rotor radius − 0.6" (pad center):
   - 6" rotor (r_eff 2.4"): F ≈ 2,700 / (2 x 0.4 x 2.4) ≈ **1,400 lb**
   - 7.1" rotor (r_eff 2.95"): F ≈ **1,150 lb**
   - 8" rotor (r_eff 3.4"): F ≈ **990 lb**
6. Can the hardware deliver it?
   - Cable mechanical caliper: ~200–400 lb clamp (est.) → ~45–115 ft-lb on a 6" rotor → **0.12–0.3 g**. Inadequate for 30 mph; acceptable for 15 mph yard use.
   - Hydraulic, single 1.00"-piston-pair caliper at 1,200 psi: ~940 lb per side → 2 x 0.4 x 940 x 2.4 ≈ 1,800 in-lb = **150 ft-lb on a 6" rotor (0.4 g)**, **~190 ft-lb on an 8" rotor (0.5 g)** – reaches the rear-tire grip limit, which is all a rear brake can do.
   - Front + rear hydraulic: total available > 300 ft-lb → tire-limited everywhere, ~0.6 g on pavement, ~50 ft stops.
7. Conclusion: hydraulic disc, 7.1"–8" rotor, 1/2"–5/8" master at ~4:1 pedal. Add fronts for hills.

## Dual rear vs front brakes
- Second rear caliper: more pad area and heat capacity, same peak deceleration. Choose when the problem is fade on long descents.
- Front brakes: raise peak deceleration from ~0.3–0.45 g to ~0.6 g; the kart stops straight instead of swapping ends. Needs front hubs with rotor flanges (5/8" or 3/4" spindle, 4-bolt), two small calipers, and ~70/30 rear/front bias (a 2-master balance bar or simply a smaller front caliper piston). Front brakes on a steering kart also need lines that flex through full lock without rubbing the tire.
- For a 250 lb driver on hills: **front brakes are the right upgrade**; a second rear caliper is the right upgrade for a flat, fast track.

## Brake lines, fluid, pedal
- Lines: -3 AN braided stainless with banjo or straight fittings (kart standard), or 3/16" hard line with a short flex section to the caliper. Never use nylon fuel tubing.
- Fluid: **check the kit**. MCP specifies DOT 5 silicone; Wilwood and most generic kits use DOT 3/4. Never mix DOT 5 with DOT 3/4.
- Route lines above the chain and away from the exhaust; P-clip every 8–10".
- Pedal: 1/4" x 1" flat or 3/4" tube, pivot in a bronze bushing on a 3/8" Grade 8 bolt, two return springs, a stop bolt so the master cylinder cannot be bottomed, and a heel-stop so your boot can't slip under the pedal.
- Bleed with the caliper higher than the master if possible; kart masters often sit low.

## Bedding in
1. Clean rotor with brake cleaner; check runout by spinning the axle (< 0.010" wobble – a bent rotor will pulse and knock the pads back).
2. 8–10 stops from ~15 mph to a walking pace, moderate pedal, never to a full stop; then 3–4 from 25–30 mph at ~70% effort.
3. Drive 1–2 minutes without braking to cool. Do not sit with the pedal held after hot stops (imprints pad material).
4. Re-torque caliper bracket bolts and hub set screws after the first ride; re-check cable adjustment (mechanical) or bleed (hydraulic) after the first week.

## Common mistakes on heavy karts
- Rotor bolted directly to a thin stamped sprocket-style hub; it warps on the first hot stop. Use a machined hub.
- Caliper bracket welded to a bearing hanger only – it rips the hanger weld under 200+ ft-lb.
- Band or mechanical brake adjusted "tight" to compensate for weak clamp; it drags, overheats, and the lining glazes within a week.
- Master cylinder mounted below the caliper with no bleed provision; air never leaves and the pedal stays spongy.
- No pedal stop: a 250 lb driver bottoms the master cylinder piston or bends the pushrod in a panic stop.
- Front brakes on a kart with slop in the spindle kingpins: the kart darts under braking. Fix the spindles first.
